Sizes and care

Measuring a room, choosing a pad, living with a rug.

A rug is bought for a floor, so the floor is where the measuring starts. This page is the room's advice on sizes, on what a pad is for, on turning a piece, and on the one household machine that does more harm to a rug than feet ever do. It ends with the list of things the room will not do.

Measuring a room for a rug

Measure the floor, not the room. Walls are not where a rug stops; the skirting, a radiator, a hearth and the swing of a door all take floor away, and the rug has to sit inside what is left. Run a tape along the two directions at floor level, write both figures down in centimetres, and note anything that intrudes.

Then decide how much floor you want to see. A rug that runs to the skirting on all sides reads as fitted carpet; a rug that stops well short of the walls reads as a rug. The band of floor showing is a matter of taste, but it should be the same on all four sides, and a hand's width is the least that reads as intentional. Take that band off both ends of each measurement and what is left is the largest rug the floor will carry.

Under a dining table the rule is different: the rug should be large enough that a chair pulled out to sit down still has all four legs on it. Measure the table, add the depth of a pulled-out chair on every side, and that is the smallest rug that works there, whatever the room around it says.

For a hallway, measure the length of floor between the two doorways or the two ends, and the width between the skirting boards. A runner stops short of both ends and leaves a band of floor along each side, and because a hand-knotted runner may not run perfectly straight, the widest point on its note is the width to plan with.

What to write down

  • The floor in both directions, in centimetres, taken down at floor level.
  • Whatever eats into it: a hearth, a radiator, the arc of a door as it opens.
  • The band of floor you have decided to keep showing.
  • For a hallway, the width between the skirting boards at the narrowest point.

Those four lines are the whole of what a reply needs in order to name the pieces that will fit.

The floor reckoner

Type the floor's length and width in centimetres, and the band of floor you want to see on each side. The reckoner takes the band off both ends of each measurement and gives the largest rug that fits, in centimetres and in feet and inches. It is subtraction and a unit change, nothing more; the judgement about the band is yours.

Two floors it cannot do

A rug under a dining table is not sized from the room. It is sized from the table plus the depth of a chair pulled out on every side, so the figure above is only the ceiling that answer has to fit under.

An L-shaped floor is measured as the rectangle you actually mean to cover. Leave the shorter arm out of the sum and treat what is left as the room.

What a pad does

A pad does three things, and only one of them is about comfort.

It holds the rug still. On a wooden or tiled floor a rug without a pad creeps with every step and ends up somewhere it was not put, and a runner in a hallway will walk the length of the corridor in a week. A pad with grip on its underside stops that.

It keeps the edges down. A rug whose corner lifts is a rug that gets caught by a foot, and a caught corner is where a selvedge starts to fail. A pad cut a little inside the rug's own edges lets those edges lie flat on the floor.

It takes wear. Every step on a rug presses the knots against whatever is underneath. Against a hard floor the foundation takes that; against a pad it is spread, and the pile lasts longer for it. On a fitted carpet the pad is thinner and its job is mostly the first one, since a rug on carpet walks even more than a rug on boards.

Pads here are cut to a piece's own measurements, a little under on each side so the pad never shows. Ask for one with the rug and it comes in the same parcel.

Turning a rug

A rug wears where it is walked on and fades where the sun lands, and neither happens evenly. Turning the rug end for end moves the walking line and the sunlit patch to the other half, and over the years that is the difference between a rug that has worn all over and one that has a bald path down its middle.

Turn it when the season changes, which is often enough to matter and rare enough to remember. If one end sits in a window, turn it more often than that. A runner in a hallway is turned end for end the same way, and it is worth turning it over too, face down for a day, so the back gets air.

Turning is also the moment to look. Lift a corner, look at the back for the pale line of a moth, run a hand along the selvedge for a place where the wrapping has opened. Anything found is a job for a repairer, caught early.

What to look at while it is up

The back, for the pale line a moth leaves along a fold. The selvedge, run through a hand, for a place where the wrapping has opened. The fringe, for warp threads gone short at one end and not the other.

Anything found goes to a repairer. Nothing found is repaired here.

Vacuuming, and what it does to fringe

A vacuum cleaner with a rotating brush is the thing in a house most likely to damage a hand-knotted rug, and the fringe is where it does it. The brush catches the free warp threads, twists them, and pulls them out of the end of the rug a few at a time; a fringe that has been vacuumed for a few years is short, ragged and taking the end of the pile with it.

So: suction only, never a beater brush, and never across the fringe at all. Vacuum the field in the direction the pile lies, which you can find by running a hand across it, the smooth way. Stop the head short of the ends. Shake the fringe out by hand, or comb it with your fingers, and leave it at that.

A flatweave has no pile and can be vacuumed on either face, but its ends and its selvedges are just as easily eaten. Cushion covers are not vacuumed; they are taken outside and shaken, or aired flat in shade.

Spills are blotted, not rubbed, and never soaked, because dyes in old wool can run and the room cannot tell you which will. Anything beyond blotting is cleaning, and cleaning is not done here.

The fringe, in one line

Fringe is the warp itself, left free where the weaving stopped. It is not sewn on afterwards and it cannot be put back by this room, which is why the rule about a beater brush is the firmest rule on the page.

What the room will not do

The room sells rugs, runners, cushion covers, pads and binding tape, and it describes them plainly. It does not offer the services that usually sit beside a rug shop, and it says so here rather than in small print.

  • We do not clean, wash, repair or restore rugs, our own or anyone else's.
  • We make no claim about a piece's age or origin as fact, and no listing will ever carry one.
  • We do not value rugs and we do not write valuations, for insurance or for sale.
  • We do not sell machine-made rugs as hand-knotted. Each listing says which it is in its first line.
  • We do not fit, lay or install anything, and we do not ship outside Canada.
